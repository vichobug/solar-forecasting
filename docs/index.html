Case study

Forecasting large solar flares from magnetogram time series

A gradient-boosted model reaches 0.84 True Skill Statistic predicting M/X-class flares from twelve hours of SHARP magnetic-field data — ahead of a 2024 contrastive-learning benchmark, and the product of finding two data bugs before finding any model improvement.

SWAN-SF benchmark dataset PyTorch · scikit-learn Source

Large solar flares are rare, costly, and — in principle — predictable from a region's magnetic field behavior in the hours before eruption. This project trains and compares five model families on the SWAN-SF benchmark, working up from aggregated summary statistics to raw sequence models. The best result, TSS 0.839 / HSS 0.216, comes from a tuned gradient-boosted tree ensemble — not the neural networks it was compared against — and depends on one dominant feature that corroborates existing solar-physics literature rather than an artifact of the pipeline.

Data

SWAN-SF: nine years of the Sun's magnetic field

An active region is a patch of the Sun's surface where the magnetic field is intensely tangled — visible as a sunspot. When that field is sheared past a breaking point, it can snap into a simpler configuration and release a flare: a burst of X-ray radiation ranked A through X by intensity, each class roughly ten times brighter than the last. Large flares (M and X class) are rare but consequential, capable of disrupting satellites, GPS, and power grids — which is also why flare datasets are heavily imbalanced by nature, not just by sampling choice.

SWAN-SF tracks this behavior via SHARP parameters — Space-weather HMI Active Region Patches, numeric summaries computed from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory magnetogram images for each active region. The full benchmark spans 4,000+ active regions and 10,000+ recorded flare events across roughly nine years (May 2010–December 2018),6 split into five non-overlapping, time-segmented partitions so that no partition leaks information about another's time period. Of the 51 available SHARP parameters, this project uses the same core set of 24 that recur across the forecasting literature: measures of total unsigned flux and stored magnetic energy, current helicity, and the sharpness of the field gradient near the polarity inversion line. That set traces back to Bobra & Couvidat's original SHARP-based flare model,4 which found several of these same quantities separated flaring from quiet regions, and was later narrowed further by Liu et al., who showed a similar subset carried most of the predictive signal for large flares specifically.5 Full definitions for all 24 parameters are in the project's data dictionary.

Each sample is one active region's twelve-hour history (60 timesteps at 12-minute cadence) leading up to a forecast point, labeled by whether a large flare followed. Training partitions are imputed, normalized, and then synthetically rebalanced toward 50/50 (random undersampling, Tomek-link cleaning, and TimeGAN oversampling) with C-class events excluded; test partitions receive only imputation and normalization, left at the natural rate — around 2% positive. That gap between an artificially balanced training world and a naturally rare test world turns out to matter more than any architecture choice that follows.

Approach

How the model evolved

A simple first pass — a couple of standard classifiers trained on hand-summarized statistics (the mean, spread, min, max, and final value of each parameter) — gave a rough sense of what was achievable. From there, two neural networks built to read the raw twelve-hour sequences directly, a 1D-CNN and an LSTM, were meant to do better by using more of the available information.

They didn't, at first — and the reason turned out to matter more than the models themselves. Early on, validation scores reached 0.90, higher than any published result on this dataset, which was itself the tell that something was off. The validation set was being drawn from the same synthetically rebalanced training data the model had learned from, so near-duplicate synthetic examples were leaking between the two. Re-checking performance on an untouched, naturally rare slice of test data brought the honest number down to 0.51 — and surfaced a second problem: the model would sometimes stop predicting the rare class at all for long stretches of training.

That turned out to be a mismatch between the world the model trained on and the world it was tested on: training data had been rebalanced to roughly even odds, while real flares stay rare, closer to one in fifty. Once the training mix and the loss function were brought back into agreement with each other, the CNN's test score rose to 0.66.

The next step paired that network with a carefully tuned gradient-boosted tree ensemble. On its own, the tuned ensemble turned out to be the strongest model tried — a test score of 0.84, ahead of the network it was paired with and everything before it, and consistent across every held-out slice of the data (0.82–0.87).

Interpretation

What the model actually uses

Permutation importance on the final model, computed on the held-out validation partition, shows one feature carrying most of the signal:

  1. R_VALUE_min0.1068
  2. R_VALUE_max0.0146
  3. TOTFX_std0.0123
  4. SAVNCPP_max0.0092
  5. MEANGBZ_max0.0070
  6. TOTFY_std0.0059

R_VALUE — unsigned flux near strong-gradient polarity inversion lines — outweighs every other feature roughly sevenfold. That it dominates is not a surprise specific to this pipeline: R_VALUE is a well-established flare precursor in the solar-physics literature,5 which is a better sign than a higher score alone would have been.
